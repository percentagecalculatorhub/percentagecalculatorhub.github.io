The Percentage Change Formula Explained

Percentage change is the difference divided by the starting value, times one hundred, and the starting value is the part people forget. This guide explains the percentage change formula explained in plain language, shows the maths with a real worked example, and points out the mistakes people make most often. You can follow along with the percentage calculator on this site, and by the end you will be able to check any result by hand and use the calculator with confidence.

How it works

To understand the percentage change formula explained, start with what the numbers mean rather than with the formula. Each input stands for something real: an amount, a size, a rate or a length of time. When you know what each one represents, the formula stops looking like a puzzle and starts looking like a description of how those things fit together. People who skip this step often enter a monthly figure where an annual one belongs, or a total where a single item price belongs, and the answer is then wrong in a way that is hard to notice.

The second thing to understand is the order of the calculation. Work out the small pieces first, keep the units consistent all the way through, and only round at the very end. Rounding in the middle can shift the final answer by more than you expect, especially when the calculation is repeated many times, as it is with monthly payments. A calculator handles the order for you, but knowing the order lets you check the result with a rough estimate in your head.

Worked example

Take a worked example. A value moves from 5 to 65. First find the difference, which is 15, then divide by the starting value of 5 and multiply by one hundred. The result is a 30 percent increase. The starting value is the base, and using the new value as the base instead would give a different and wrong answer. As a check, apply the percentage back: 5 plus 30 percent of 5 is 65, which returns to the new value when the change is an increase. Always state whether a change is an increase or a decrease, because the sign carries the meaning.

Common mistakes

Three errors show up again and again. People use the wrong base value, so a percentage is calculated from the new number instead of the old one. People add percentages that should be multiplied, which overstates the result. And people forget that a fee, a tax or a charge may be added after the calculation, so the real total is higher than the calculator shows.

  • Entering numbers in the wrong units or the wrong order.
  • Rounding in the middle of the calculation instead of at the end.
  • Trusting the first result without a rough check against common sense.

Practical tips

A few habits make the tool far more useful. Measure rather than guess, write the numbers down before typing them in, and run the calculation twice with the same inputs to catch slips. Keep a note of the result and the date so you can compare it next time, and re-check whenever something changes, such as a new rate or a different amount. Small, regular checks beat one big calculation every time.

Tip: write down the numbers you entered and the date. A result without its inputs is impossible to check later, and a dated note turns one calculation into a useful record.

Using the change formula well

Percentage change always compares the difference with the starting value, never with the new one. A price that rises from 40 to 50 has increased by 10, and 10 divided by 40 is 25 percent, so the rise is 25 percent. Using the new price of 50 as the base would give 20 percent, which is a different question and a wrong answer for a rise from 40.

The same rule applies to falls. A score that drops from 80 to 60 has fallen by 20, and 20 divided by 80 is 25 percent, so the fall is 25 percent.
